## Instructions

This reflective writing assignment enables you to demonstrate how your review of academic literature and discussion with your supervisor influenced the shaping of your project topic, methodology, and planning direction. It also provides a structured opportunity to identify what you have learned, what challenges you faced, and what skills or tools you still need to develop. The reflection acts as a critical link between your literature review and your formal project planning document, justifying your decisions and clarifying your learning process.

### Your Reflective Writing Should:

#### 1. Show how academic literature shaped your project
Explain how prior research influenced your:
- Topic definition and problem framing
- Project scope and objectives
- Methodological decisions (e.g., modelling tools, indicators, case studies)

#### 2. Demonstrate how you integrated supervisor input
- Meet your academic supervisor to discuss your literature findings and planning ideas
- Mention the meeting dates in your reflection and enclose at least TWO meeting logs (see Appendix A for the meeting log template)

#### 3. Include the following reflective elements

**Challenges faced** in reviewing literature, refining your topic, or aligning theory with practice—and how you addressed them

**Lessons learned**—intellectually, practically, or personally—from the literature review process or supervisor discussion

**Technical Tools and Skills Gap Analysis**:
- Identify key tools (e.g., software, development platform, data analysis packages) or techniques needed for your project
- Assess your current level of proficiency
- Outline how you plan to acquire or improve these skills (if needed)

#### 4. General writing requirements
- Use at least 5–8 academic references, properly cited
- Write in a structured and concise manner, balancing personal reflection with academic reasoning

# Appendix B: Guidelines for Reflective Writing

The reflection connects your literature review to your project planning document. It should demonstrate critical thinking, integration of supervisor feedback, awareness of technical skill needs, and personal learning.

## Key Principles

- **Define your purpose clearly**: Briefly introduce your project focus and explain how this reflection supports your planning process.
- **Engage critically with literature**: Identify key academic sources that shaped your problem definition, methodology, or scope.
- **Justify planning choices**: Show how your choices (e.g., methods, algorithms, tools, datasets) were informed by literature and supervisor input.
- **Reflect on challenges**: Describe real difficulties encountered (e.g., model selection, data issues) and how you addressed them.
- **Show intellectual growth**: Explain how your thinking evolved through research or supervisory discussions.
- **Analyse technical skills and tools**: Identify required tools (e.g., Python libraries, platforms, frameworks), evaluate your proficiency, and outline a plan to upskill.
- **Maintain an academic tone**: Write clearly and professionally while being honest and reflective.
- **Include supervisor insights**: Summarise how your supervisor's feedback shaped your direction. Attach the meeting log.
- **Respect the word count**: Be concise, structured, and relevant.

## Sample Reflective Writing (Example 1)

**Project Topic:** Assessing the Role of Urban Green Infrastructure in Reducing Surface Runoff in Kuala Lumpur

**Supervisor Meeting Dates:** 5 January 2025, 12 February 2025, 1 March 2025

**Meeting Logs attached:** Appendix A

### Body

Engaging with the academic literature on urban green infrastructure (UGI) and stormwater management has significantly refined both the scope and methodology of my capstone project. My original idea was to assess the impact of rainfall intensity on urban flooding, focusing primarily on precipitation data and drainage system analysis. However, early readings—especially Gill et al. (2007), Fletcher et al. (2015), and Ahiablame et al. (2012)—shifted my perspective toward sustainable and nature-based solutions, specifically the hydrological and ecological role of UGI.

Fletcher et al. (2015) present a compelling argument for integrated green-grey systems, where bioswales, green roofs, and permeable pavements work alongside traditional drainage to reduce runoff. This reshaped my understanding of urban flood management: rather than evaluating drainage efficiency in isolation, my study should investigate how green infrastructure supplements existing systems. I discussed this revised focus with my supervisor during our 5 April meeting, and she supported the direction, suggesting I narrow my geographic scope to two contrasting neighbourhoods to allow for comparative analysis.

Following this advice, I selected Bukit Jalil and Taman Melati, two residential areas in Kuala Lumpur that differ in population density, elevation, and built environment characteristics. The literature review highlighted how local land use affects UGI performance (Yang & Li, 2021), leading me to compare UGI effectiveness across different urban contexts. This directly informed the sampling strategy and evaluation criteria proposed in my project planning document.

A major turning point in my planning came from Liu and Jensen's (2018) simulation-based assessment using the EPA Storm Water Management Model (SWMM). Their study showed that SWMM could accurately simulate runoff volumes for different green infrastructure scenarios. While I had originally anticipated collecting field data, the literature revealed significant limitations for field-based hydrology in the short term—especially for a 3–4-month capstone. My supervisor endorsed the simulation approach, advising me to use SWMM to model current runoff levels (baseline) and compare them with projected outcomes after implementing UGI features. This change made the project more feasible and technically rigorous.

However, this shift presented challenges. I had no prior experience using SWMM and found the model's structure, parameters, and input requirements initially overwhelming. I attempted to self-learn using EPA documentation, but progress was slow. My supervisor recommended a MOOC course on urban hydrological modelling, which I began in mid-April. The technical tools and skills gap became a key reflection point: although I have a strong environmental science background, my proficiency in simulation tools and GIS integration was limited. As a result, I created an upskilling plan, which includes:

- Completing the online SWMM module by early May
- Practicing with sample datasets from previous published studies (e.g., from Liu & Jensen, 2018)
- Attending GIS lab sessions at the faculty to improve integration of spatial runoff maps

Through this, I learned that skills acquisition is not a barrier but a necessary part of designing robust environmental assessments.

Another challenge I faced was distinguishing between different types of green infrastructure (GI) in the literature. Some sources use "UGI" broadly, while others classify interventions into structural vs. non-structural (Berland et al., 2017). This made it difficult to define consistent variables for analysis. After discussing this issue with my supervisor, she advised me to adopt the classification framework used by Salim et al. (2020), which categorises GI elements by scale (parcel-level, street-scale, and neighbourhood-scale). This framework provided clarity and will be used in both the literature matrix and the planning document.

Reflecting on the entire process, I gained several insights. First, a literature review is not just a preliminary phase—it's the intellectual foundation that determines the credibility and feasibility of project planning. I learned that careful evaluation of methodologies, outcome measures, and context-specific insights is essential for informed decision-making. Second, the supervisor meeting was instrumental not just for validation, but for identifying blind spots in my plan. For example, my initial focus was exclusively hydrological, but she encouraged me to include co-benefits like temperature regulation and community acceptance in my planning. This prompted me to include a stakeholder feedback component (via short surveys) in the project, based on suggestions from Tzoulas et al. (2007) and Wong et al. (2020).

Lastly, I've come to understand that a reflective process—when combined with a structured literature review and responsive supervision—produces a planning document that is not only academically grounded, but also practically viable. My current plan includes both a simulation-based runoff assessment and a qualitative evaluation of perceived UGI benefits, drawing directly from what I've read, learned, and discussed.

In summary, this reflection helped me critically align theory, context, and planning. Literature shaped the what and why of my project; supervision shaped the how; and my own learning process shaped the readiness. As I finalise my planning document, I am confident that the decisions made are not arbitrary, but are justified by credible evidence, contextual needs, and thoughtful revision.

## Sample Reflective Writing (Example 2)

**Project Topic:** Evaluating the Impact of Community-Based Nutrition Interventions on Childhood Obesity in Urban Malaysia

**Supervisor Meeting Dates:** 12 March 2025, 19 March 2025, 25 March 2025, 2 April 2025

**Meeting Log Attached:** Appendix A

### Body

This reflection captures the key ways in which academic literature and supervisory consultation shaped the development of my capstone project, particularly how the literature review process directly influenced my project planning decisions. It also includes the challenges encountered, lessons learned, and a critical self-assessment of the technical and methodological skills required for implementation.

Initially, my project proposal focused on school-based nutrition campaigns to reduce childhood obesity in Kuala Lumpur. I intended to evaluate health education sessions and in-school food policies. However, upon conducting a targeted literature review, particularly drawing from Swinburn et al. (2019) and Story et al. (2008), I began to see that obesity is not just an outcome of poor dietary behaviour at the individual level, but the result of deeper structural factors, including food accessibility, marketing exposure, and neighbourhood design.

Swinburn et al. (2019) introduced the concept of the "obesogenic environment," emphasising that policy-level and environmental factors shape behaviour far more than education alone. That insight led me to reframe my project to focus on community-based interventions—programmes that modify the local environment, improve access to healthy food, or encourage multi-stakeholder collaboration.

During a supervisor consultation held on 12 March 2025, I presented my revised direction. My supervisor agreed with the reframing, especially noting that such a project would have more real-world applicability. However, she also cautioned me to ensure the project remained feasible within the time and data constraints. She suggested I focus on a limited number of community interventions with well-documented objectives or outcomes.

I reviewed several studies following this advice. Bleich et al. (2013) conducted a systematic review and found that multi-sectoral programmes—those involving schools, local governments, and NGOs—tend to produce more sustainable results. This helped me finalise the scope of my planning document: I would evaluate two interventions—one led by a city council and another by a grassroots health NGO—in two different urban districts.

The challenge I encountered next was identifying consistent outcome indicators for comparison. Many published interventions report on different metrics (e.g., BMI z-scores, food purchasing patterns, participation rates), which made it difficult to design a uniform evaluation framework. After reviewing Glasgow et al.'s (1999) RE-AIM framework, which provides a structured approach for evaluating public health programmes (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance), I decided to adopt this model in my project planning document.

However, integrating the RE-AIM model required a stronger grasp of qualitative research methods—particularly for evaluating Adoption and Maintenance components through stakeholder interviews. I had limited formal training in qualitative analysis, which I raised with my supervisor. She recommended two courses offered by the department and advised me to review previous student theses that used the same framework. I enrolled in the research design module's qualitative methods workshop and began reviewing recent qualitative capstone examples. This formed part of my technical tools and skills gap analysis: while I am confident in quantitative data interpretation, I required support in:

- Semi-structured interview design
- Thematic coding using NVivo
- Mapping qualitative responses to RE-AIM dimensions

To address this gap, I developed a personal upskilling plan:

- Complete the department's NVivo workshop by early April
- Use a sample interview protocol from a published study (Wong et al., 2021) to test interview logic
- Conduct a pilot interview with a dietitian and use the transcript to practice manual thematic coding

The literature review also taught me the value of considering equity and cultural relevance in intervention evaluation. Articles by Kumanyika (2019) and Rahim et al. (2021) discussed how interventions designed in Western contexts may be ineffective or even counterproductive in urban Southeast Asia. This prompted me to include a cultural sensitivity criterion in my planning document, ensuring that questions and indicators are adapted for the local context. I learned that standardising instruments (like 24-hour dietary recalls) without cultural adjustments could misrepresent results, especially in multi-ethnic communities.

One of the unexpected lessons during the literature review was the limited documentation of Malaysian community programmes, especially post-intervention evaluations. While I found descriptive reports or policy briefs, few offered robust methodological details. This lack of local data reinforced the importance of my project and helped me justify its contribution as part of the planning document's rationale. As my supervisor noted, contributing even a modest, contextually grounded evaluation would help fill a visible gap in national public health research.

Reflecting on this process, I've learned that literature reviews are not passive background exercises. They are active agents in shaping and re-shaping research direction. Initially, I underestimated how much my project would evolve from interacting with studies and feedback. I entered the project with a prescriptive idea about "what works," but the literature taught me to be more flexible, evidence-based, and realistic.

Furthermore, I realised that reflective writing itself is a powerful learning tool. Articulating my decision-making process, skill needs, and intellectual shifts has helped me internalise and organise my planning steps more clearly. Importantly, integrating supervisor guidance into this reflection provided a checkpoint for feasibility and academic rigor.

In summary, my capstone planning is now grounded in a critical reading of both global and local research, tempered by supervisory input, and supported by an intentional upskilling strategy. I believe this reflection not only justifies the decisions outlined in my planning document but also highlights my readiness to carry them out.

**Word Count:** 947
